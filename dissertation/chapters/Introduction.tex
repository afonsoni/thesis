\chapter{Introduction} \label{ch:introduction}

\section{Context}

In recent years, Large Language Models (LLMs) have transformed natural language processing (NLP). Once instruction tuning turned them into chatbots \citep{NEURIPS2022_b1efde53}, they became capable of summarising, answering questions and analysing text \citep{openai2024gpt4technicalreport, yang2023harnessingpowerllmspractice}. They also have a well-known limitation: what a model knows comes from its training data, and it learns popular facts well and rare ones poorly \citep{kandpal2023largelanguagemodelsstruggle}. Asked about something outside that data, or more recent than it, a model still answers, fluently and sometimes wrongly \citep{rag-survey}. The manifestos of Portuguese parties for the 2024 election are exactly this kind of niche, recent knowledge that a model is unlikely to have seen.

This matters because manifestos matter. They are the documents in which a party sets out its policy positions and ideological stances \citep{eder2017manifesto}. Nor are they empty words: parties that reach government keep a large share of their pledges \citep{thomson2017pledges}. They are also long: the eight manifestos of the 2024 Portuguese legislative election add up to almost 1{,}400 pages, from 94 for the shortest to 275 for the longest. A voter who wants to compare what each party proposes for housing has to read through eight of them.

\section{Motivation}

Tools to navigate and compare party manifestos already exist, but each has a limitation. Political-science methods turn a manifesto into a position on a scale or a set of topic labels \citep{laver_benoit_garry, manifestos-nz}: useful to a researcher, impossible for a voter to check. Newspaper comparators put the parties side by side \citep{expresso-comparador, politicaativa-comparar}, but someone wrote every cell by hand, so they answer only the questions their editors thought of. A general-purpose chatbot takes any question and answers it from memory, which, as seen above, is where the manifestos are least likely to be.

This work proposes \sysname{}, a system that uses Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) \citep{rag-nlp} to find what the manifestos actually say and answer from that text, with every claim pointing back to the passage it came from. It does so in European Portuguese, which has fewer resources than English: a Portuguese benchmark for embedding models appeared only in 2026 \citep{okamura2026mtebpt}, and earlier work on Portuguese manifestos measured sentiment rather than answering questions \citep{marino-etal-2024-decoding}.

The political context adds to the case. The Portuguese voting attitude has become more volatile over the last two decades \citep{eleitorado}, and the 2024 election reflected it: eight parties won seats, and far-right party Chega went from 12 to 50 members of parliament \citep{cne2024mapa}, challenging the long dominance of PS and PSD. More parties means more manifestos to read, and more reliance on second-hand summaries of them. A tool that grounds its answers in the parties' own words can contribute to a better-informed electorate. And because a manifesto does not change after the election, it lets anyone check, years later, whether a party's claims about what it always stood for match what it actually wrote.

\section{Objectives}

The main objective was to find out how well RAG retrieves and synthesises political content, using the manifestos of the 2024 Portuguese legislative election as the test case. It broke down into three goals:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{RAG implementations:} survey RAG techniques and test which of them work on political manifestos.
    \item \textbf{Challenges of RAG:} identify what makes Portuguese manifestos hard for a RAG system.
    \item \textbf{Solutions to challenges:} apply and measure techniques that address those challenges, such as token-sized chunking, contextual retrieval, hybrid dense and lexical search, and reranking.
\end{itemize}

The work delivered four things:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{\sysname{}}, a working prototype that answers questions about the eight manifestos, citing the passages each claim comes from (Chapters~\ref{ch:proposition} and~\ref{ch:prototype-demonstration}).
    \item \textbf{A step-by-step evaluation}: retrieval techniques are added one at a time, each behind its own switch, so each one's contribution can be measured on its own (Section~\ref{subsec:variants}).
    \item \textbf{An evaluation harness and gold set}: 61 questions written and reviewed by hand, each anchored to the manifesto passages that answer it, scored with retrieval, routing and generation metrics (Sections~\ref{subsec:goldset} and~\ref{subsec:metrics}).
    \item \textbf{A decision history}: for each component, the options considered, the one picked and, when a choice was later overturned, why (Chapter~\ref{ch:proposition}).
\end{itemize}

A RAG-powered political quiz, to improve on the quizzes that appear in every election campaign, was an optional goal. It was not built and is discussed as future work (Chapter~\ref{ch:conclusion}).

\section{Research Hypothesis}

The main hypothesis of this project is:

“RAG-based retrieval and synthesis can effectively improve the accessibility and interpretability of Portuguese political manifestos.”

Put simply: given the manifestos, a RAG system should find the passages that matter and summarise them faithfully enough to give a voter a structured, checkable overview of where each party stands.

\section{Research Questions} \label{sec:research-questions}

The hypothesis is too broad to test directly, so it is split into five questions the evaluation harness can answer. Each maps to a part of \sysname{}: RQ1 to indexing, RQ2 to retrieval, RQ3 to generation, and RQ4 and RQ5 to the system as a whole.

\begin{description}
    \item[RQ1 (Representation).] How do representation choices, such as measuring
    chunk size in the encoder's own tokens and enriching each chunk with its
    section path and an LLM-written context, affect retrieval quality over
    Portuguese electoral manifestos?

    \item[RQ2 (Retrieval).] Which retrieval techniques (structured query
    analysis with routing, multi-query expansion with rank fusion, hybrid
    dense and lexical search, cross-encoder reranking) improve retrieval over
    a naive baseline, and how much does each contribute on its own?

    \item[RQ3 (Generation).] Do retrieval improvements translate into
    better answers, measured as faithfulness to the retrieved manifesto
    content, relevance to the question, and correctness against a reference
    answer?

    \item[RQ4 (Cost).] What do these improvements cost in latency, tokens
    and money, and which of them are worth that cost for a tool intended for
    public use?

    \item[RQ5 (Retrieval versus scale).] How does \sysname{} compare with
    asking a language model the same question without retrieval, in answer
    quality and in resources consumed? Does a small model with the manifestos
    match or beat a stronger model without them, or a commercial assistant
    with web search?
\end{description}

\section{Research Methods}

The research followed an experimental approach: build, measure, change one component, measure again. The plan had the following steps:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Literature review:} a review of research on RAG pipelines, political text analysis, and the related challenges in NLP.
    \item \textbf{Dataset compilation:} collecting and preprocessing the 2024 Portuguese legislative manifestos.
    \item \textbf{Development:} building \sysname{} and testing RAG configurations on it.
    \item \textbf{Testing and evaluation:}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Quantitative methods:} retrieval metrics (hit rate, recall, mean reciprocal rank, nDCG) against hand-picked gold passages, and claim-level answer metrics scored by an LLM judge \citep{es2024ragas, zheng2023judging}.
        \item \textbf{Qualitative methods:} reading answers for errors the metrics cannot see, such as political framing passing from the sources into the answer (Section~\ref{subsec:grounding}).
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Refinement:} improving \sysname{} based on the evaluation results.
\end{itemize}

The plan held, but the process was not linear. The field moved fast while this work ran (Section~\ref{sec:sota-rag}), and several early choices were revisited: the embedding model, the vector database, and the unit in which chunks are measured. Changing course when better evidence came up was part of the method, not a departure from it. Chapter~\ref{ch:proposition} records every reversal and its reason, and because each technique can be switched on and off on its own, every reversal is a measured change rather than a guess.

The research question itself changed even earlier. The work began as an attempt to place Portuguese political text, from manifestos to news articles, on three axes (social, economic, and libertarian versus authoritarian) with a fine-tuned LLM. It was dropped because political bias has no agreed ground truth, so a classifier's verdict would be as hard to check as the scales discussed above, and because no labelled Portuguese dataset of the needed size existed. Narrowing the work to manifestos, and to questions whose answers are in the text, gave it something it could measure, a passage that either supports a claim or does not. The original concern did not go away. A system that only reports what parties wrote can still repeat how they wrote it, and Section~\ref{subsec:grounding} measures that framing leakage.
