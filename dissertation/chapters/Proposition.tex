Indexing turns the eight manifesto PDFs into a searchable store in seven
steps: each document is parsed to Markdown, cleaned, split into chunks,
labelled with policy topics, given a short context of its own, embedded, and
written to a vector database.
It runs offline, once per corpus change, and everything the retrieval
pipeline (Section~\ref{sec:retrieval}) does depends on the representation it
produces. The subsections below follow the pipeline in order
(Figure~\ref{fig:indexing}). Each one states
the problem, the options considered, the choice made, and, where it happened,
how and why that choice was later revised.

    \caption{The indexing pipeline. Document preparation runs once per
    manifesto and its output is reviewed by hand; the indexing pipeline then
    chunks, classifies, contextualises and embeds the cleaned Markdown into the

\subsection{Corpus} \label{subsec:corpus}

The corpus consists of the electoral manifestos published for the 2024
Portuguese legislative election by the eight parties already represented in
the Assembleia da República before that election: Aliança Democrática (AD), Partido Socialista
(PS), CHEGA, Iniciativa Liberal (IL), Bloco de Esquerda (BE), Partido
Comunista Português (PCP), LIVRE and Pessoas--Animais--Natureza (PAN).
PSD sat in the outgoing parliament on its own and stood in 2024 within the
AD coalition. All eight were re-elected, so the corpus also covers every party
in the parliament that resulted. The 2024 election was chosen because it was
under way when this work began. Limiting the corpus to one election and to
parties already in parliament kept the manual work of preparing documents
manageable. The cost is that parties on the ballot but outside parliament,
often the ones voters know least about, are not represented. The indexing
pipeline was later made easier to extend for this reason
(Section~\ref{subsec:cleaning}). Every chunk also records the election year,
so manifestos from other elections can sit alongside these. That would make
longitudinal questions possible, such as how a party's position on a given
issue changed between elections (Chapter~\ref{ch:conclusion}). AD and PCP ran as coalitions (PSD/CDS-PP/PPM and CDU
respectively); the documents used are the programmes those coalitions
published.

uneven, ranging from 94 to 275 pages and from about 31 to 68 thousand words
after conversion, which totals roughly 1{,}400 pages and 427 thousand words.

\subsection{Document parsing} \label{subsec:parsing}

Manifestos are written to be printed and read, not parsed. They use
multi-column layouts, decorative title pages, running page headers, pull
quotes, tables of contents and infographics. A text extractor must recover
the running text in reading order and, ideally, the heading structure that
organises it into chapters and sections.

Five tools were identified as candidates during the preliminary research:
\href{https://pymupdf.readthedocs.io/en/latest/}{PyMuPDF},
\href{https://github.com/pdfminer/pdfminer.six}{pdfminer.six},
\href{https://pypi.org/project/pypdf/}{PyPDF},
\href{https://docs.llamaindex.ai/en/stable/llama_cloud/llama_parse/}{LlamaParse}
and Marker~\citep{marker}. Of these,
pdfminer.six and PyMuPDF were tried first (January 2025), as both are
widely used plain-text extractors, and Microsoft's
MarkItDown~\citep{markitdown} was tried as well. None extracted the text
reliably enough to avoid substantial manual repair. LlamaParse was ruled out
without being tried because it is a paid cloud service. No record survives of
PyPDF being tested.

Marker was adopted in April 2025 and all eight manifestos were converted with
it. It converts PDF directly to Markdown using neural layout-detection and
OCR models, runs locally, and is free for research use. It also offers an
LLM-assisted mode, which was not used. Its code is licensed under the GPL,
while its model weights carry a modified OpenRAIL-M licence that permits
research and personal use. Direct
Markdown output turned out to matter more than expected. Markdown headings
are what the chunker later uses to split documents along their own sections
(Section~\ref{subsec:chunking}), so the parser's ability to recover headings
ended up being load-bearing for retrieval. The selection was pragmatic.
Parsing is a means here, not the object of study, and Marker was the first
tool that met the requirement. No systematic comparison of parsers was run.

The corrections fall into three kinds. First, and most important, is the
heading hierarchy. Marker frequently promoted running page headers and
section titles to top-level headings; the LIVRE manifesto came out with 57 of
them, one for nearly every page. Each document was restored to a single title
and a consistent tree of sections beneath it. Second, tables of contents
were removed: Marker renders them as Markdown tables of section names and page
numbers, which match almost any query while containing no proposals. Third,
layout residue was removed: image links, page anchors, repeated page headers
and fragmented cover titles. Proposal text was left alone; between 1\% and
4\% of words were removed, almost all of them from tables of contents and
page furniture.

Hand correction took twenty to thirty minutes per manifesto and was done for
four of the eight. That cost is acceptable once but does not scale to new
election cycles, to earlier manifestos, or to parties outside parliament.
In October 2026 the remaining four manifestos were therefore cleaned with an
automated cleaner written for the purpose, followed by a manual review. It runs
in two passes. A deterministic pass removes the residue described above. An
LLM pass then decides only the heading structure: the model is shown the
existing heading candidates and, for each one, keeps it at a given level,
demotes it to body text, or drops it. A verification step rejects any output
containing a word absent from the input, so the model cannot paraphrase or
invent manifesto text. The manual review still had to fix part and chapter
headings the model missed in all four documents.

\subsection{Chunking} \label{subsec:chunking}

Retrieval operates on chunks, not documents, so chunk size sets the
granularity of everything downstream. Small chunks give precise matches and
more diverse sources but lose surrounding context. Large chunks keep context
but dilute the embedding and risk burying the relevant sentence in the middle
of the passage (Table~\ref{tab:chunk-sizes}).

\begin{table}[h]
    \centering
    \begin{tabular}{|c|c|}
        \hline
        \textbf{Small chunks} & \textbf{Large chunks}\\
        \hline
        More granular matches & Less granular matches \\
        More diverse sources & Fewer, longer sources \\
        Incomplete context & Relevant text lost in the middle \\
        \hline
    \end{tabular}
    \caption{Trade-offs in chunk size.}
    \label{tab:chunk-sizes}
\end{table}

The final design combines three ideas. First, documents are split along their
own Markdown headings, so every chunk belongs to exactly one section and
carries that section's path (for example \textit{Com Sensibilidade Social
$>$ Saúde $>$ Medidas}) as metadata. Second, sections longer than the budget
are windowed recursively at paragraph, then sentence, then word boundaries,
with overlap between consecutive windows. Third, the windows are retrieved
small-to-big: the small window is what gets embedded and matched, while the
full parent section is what the generator reads. The current budget is 400
tokens per window with an 80-token overlap, which yields 383 chunks for the
AD manifesto.

\paragraph{How the design was reached.} Chunk size was revised four times.
The first prototype (May 2025) split text by words.
Its configuration asked for 512 words with an overlap of 200, but the code
never read that setting and used its own defaults of 1{,}000 words with 200
of overlap. The encoder at the time, \texttt{all-MiniLM-L6-v2}, reads at most
256 tokens, so truncation was already present in the first prototype, a year
before it was found. The rewrite of March 2026 switched to 1{,}000 characters
with 100 of overlap, and in April 2026 this grew to 1{,}600 characters with
200 of overlap. The increases followed practitioner guidance at the time,
which moved towards larger chunks as LLM context windows grew. One widely
read benchmark found page-level chunks gave the best average answer accuracy
across its datasets~\citep{nvidia-chunking}. The evidence is more nuanced
than the trend, however. A multi-dataset study found that small chunks
(64--128 tokens) suit questions with short factual answers, while larger ones
(512--1{,}024 tokens) help only when answers need broader
context~\citep{bhat2025rethinkingchunksize}.

The flaw in these choices only appeared in August 2026, when chunk lengths
were first measured in the unit the embedding model actually uses. The
encoder then in use, serafim-900m (Section~\ref{subsec:embeddings}),
accepts at most 128 tokens and silently drops the rest. At 1{,}600
characters the median chunk was 365 tokens: 89.5\% of chunks were truncated,
and only 37.3\% of the corpus text was ever embedded. A probe made the cost
concrete. Queried with its own opening, a chunk ranked first (cosine
similarity 0.986). Queried with its own closing text, it did not appear in the
top five. Two thirds of each chunk was invisible to retrieval, and nothing in
the pipeline reported an error.

There is an irony here worth stating plainly. The research plan written
before implementation had already proposed a chunk-size experiment measured
in tokens, from 128 to 2{,}048. The implementation drifted to characters
because that is the default unit of the common splitting libraries, and the
plan's own unit would have exposed the problem at once.

The diagnosis was that one number had been made to serve two budgets.
The \emph{retrieval budget} is set by the encoder's maximum input length,
and exceeding it truncates silently. The \emph{generation budget} is set by
the LLM's context window, and at these sizes it is never exceeded. The
character-sized chunks had been sized for the second budget and fed to a
component bound by the first. Two fixes followed. Chunk length is now
measured with the embedding model's own tokenizer, so the budget is expressed
in the unit that constrains it. Small-to-big retrieval decouples the two
budgets entirely: the embedded window can be small and precise while the
generator still receives the full section. After the change, no AD chunk was
truncated and the whole corpus was embedded. The character-based strategy is
kept only as the baseline against which this is measured.

\subsection{Topic classification} \label{subsec:classification}

Query-time routing (Section~\ref{subsec:routing}) needs to know what each
chunk is about, so that a question on housing can be restricted to housing
passages, or a topic can be explored across parties. No labelled Portuguese
manifesto data exists for this, and none of the documents tag their own
sections consistently. Topics are therefore assigned by an LLM during indexing.

\paragraph{Taxonomy.} The 18 categories (Table~\ref{tab:topics} in
Appendix~\ref{app:results}) were derived by reading the
manifestos for recurring policy areas and by consulting two public online
tools for comparing party programmes, the \textit{Expresso} manifesto
comparator for the 2024 election~\citep{expresso-comparador} and
\textit{PolíticaAtivaPT}~\citep{politicaativa-comparar}. The final list
combines categories from both. Classification is multi-label because manifesto passages often
address several areas at once. A proposal to build student residences
concerns housing, education and youth. Exempting young buyers from property
transfer tax concerns housing, taxation and youth.

\paragraph{Method.} Chunks are classified in batches of 15 through structured
output: the model returns, for each chunk, a list of categories, a confidence
score and keywords, validated against a schema. The model is
\texttt{gemma-4-31b-it}, an open-weights model served free of charge through
OpenRouter.

\paragraph{Revisions.} Three changes followed from running the classifier at
scale.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Fail fast, resume cheaply.} The first version fell back to
    keyword matching whenever an LLM call failed. Under free-tier rate limits
    that happened often, and it produced a silent mix of LLM labels and much
    cruder substring-match labels, with nothing to tell them apart. The
    fallback was removed in April 2026. A failed call now stops the run, and a
    checkpoint lets it resume where it stopped, so a stored label is always an
    LLM label.
    \item \textbf{Optional classification.} Classification costs roughly
    thirty minutes of rate-limited calls per manifesto, so in August 2026 it
    became optional. Retrieval experiments that do not depend on topics
    (chunking, encoders, hybrid search) can then re-index without spending
    that quota.
    \item \textbf{Fewer, better labels.} A manual check of 30 random AD chunks
    (October 2026) found the first label correct in about 29 cases. However,
    about 8 chunks carried a clearly wrong additional label (tourism tagged
    as health, business financing as public finance), and about 6 more carried
    a reflexive \textit{governance} label whenever the State was mentioned.
    The self-reported confidence was 4 or 5 out of 5 on all 383 chunks, so it
    could not be used to filter. The cause was the prompt, which asked for
    ``multiple categories (up to four)''. It now asks for the main topic
    first, adds a further label only when the passage contains a concrete
    proposal or figure on it, and caps labels at three.

\end{itemize}

\subsection{Contextual retrieval} \label{subsec:contextual}

A chunk cut from the middle of a section is an orphan. It no longer says which
party wrote it or what the section is about. In this corpus, 58\% of chunks
(1{,}635 of 2{,}815) contain no heading at all. Voters do not help either: they
rarely use the manifesto's own words.

Contextual retrieval~\citep{anthropic2024contextual} gives each orphan a
label. Before embedding, an LLM reads the chunk alongside its section and
writes a sentence or two placing it in context. The chunk is then embedded,
and indexed for lexical search, together with that context and its
``party | section'' path. The original text is stored untouched, so citations
and the gold set still point at exactly what the manifesto says. Contexts are
cached by content hash, so a rebuild never pays for them twice, and, like
classification, a context that cannot be written stops the run.

It paid off. Over all 61 gold questions, run twice, the contextual index beat
the plain hybrid one on recall (+0.046, 95\% CI [+0.011, +0.089]), MRR
(+0.082) and nDCG (+0.057, CI [+0.025, +0.095]). Section paths alone gave
about half of that. Since October 2026 it is the default index.

\subsection{Embeddings} \label{subsec:embeddings}

The embedding model decides which chunks a query can find. Three properties
matter for this corpus. The model must handle European Portuguese well, it
must be trained for \emph{retrieval} (matching a short question to a longer
passage that answers it) rather than for sentence similarity, and its input
window must fit the chunk budget.

\paragraph{Candidates.} The preliminary research used the Massive Text
Embedding Benchmark (MTEB)~\cite{mtebmassivetextembedding} to shortlist
models supporting Portuguese: BERTimbau~\citep{souza2020bertimbau}, Sentence-BERT~\citep{reimers2019sentencebert},
\texttt{paraphrase-multilingual-MiniLM-L12-v2},
\texttt{multilingual-e5-large-instruct}~\cite{wang2024multilinguale5textembeddings},
and two commercial APIs (OpenAI's \texttt{text-embedding-3-large} and Google's
\texttt{text-multilingual-embedding-002}). The commercial options were set
aside so that \sysname{} could run entirely locally on open models.

\paragraph{First choices.} The first prototype (May 2025) used
\texttt{all-MiniLM-L6-v2}, an English-only model chosen to get the
pipeline running end to end rather than for quality. Results were poor, as
expected, and the encoder was the first component revisited. In August 2025
it was replaced by \texttt{serafim-900m-portuguese-pt-sentence-encoder-ir}
from the Serafim family of European Portuguese
encoders~\citep{gomes2024serafim}. Serafim was attractive on every visible criterion: trained on
European Portuguese specifically, released in an information-retrieval
variant, recent, and near the top of the Portuguese leaderboards. It was the
first encoder with which \sysname{}'s answers became acceptable.

\paragraph{Reversal.} Serafim had one property that was not noticed at
selection time: it reads at most 128 tokens and silently discards the rest,
which caused the truncation described in Section~\ref{subsec:chunking}. The
limit is not a configuration detail that could simply be raised. The model
was trained on short inputs, so there is no evidence it would represent a
365-token passage well even if allowed to read one. In August 2026 it was
replaced by \texttt{Qwen3-Embedding-0.6B}~\citep{zhang2025qwen3embedding},
a decoder-based encoder trained for retrieval that accepts inputs of up to 32
thousand tokens. The encoder therefore no longer constrains chunk size at
all. It is also smaller (0.6B against 0.9B parameters) and produces narrower
vectors (1{,}024 against 1{,}536 dimensions). On MTEB-PT, a Portuguese
benchmark published after the switch, it scores 78.9 on
retrieval~\citep{okamura2026mtebpt}. That benchmark evaluates only the
general-purpose Serafim sentence encoder (54.9 on retrieval, 86.7 on semantic
similarity), not the retrieval-tuned variant used here, so it offers no
like-for-like comparison. The encoder comparison planned on this corpus is
what will provide one.

The replacement also brought a change in how the model is used. Like most
current retrieval encoders, Qwen3-Embedding is \emph{asymmetric}: queries are
prefixed with an instruction, while documents are encoded as they are. \sysname{}
therefore encodes queries and documents through separate paths, so the
instruction is never omitted at search time.

The move from a Portuguese-native model to a multilingual one is a trade-off,
not a straightforward upgrade. A model not built around Portuguese may
represent the language's ambiguities less well, for example homonyms such as
\textit{canto} (``corner'' or ``I sing'') or \textit{rio} (``river'' or ``I
laugh''). Whether a long-window Portuguese encoder would outperform Qwen3 on
this corpus is an empirical question. The evaluation harness can answer it by
re-indexing the corpus once per encoder and comparing retrieval metrics on the
same gold set (Section~\ref{sec:evaluation}). \TODO{Report the encoder
comparison once run, or move it to future work.}

\subsection{Vector store} \label{subsec:vectorstore}

The store must hold one vector per chunk alongside its metadata (party,
section path, topics), and answer nearest-neighbour queries filtered by that
metadata.

\paragraph{Candidates and first choice.} The preliminary research surveyed
dedicated vector databases (Faiss, Pinecone, Qdrant, Milvus, ChromaDB and
Weaviate) with reference to ANN-Benchmarks~\citep{aumuller2020annbenchmarks}. All but Pinecone are
open source. Milvus was chosen first (May 2025), on the strength of its
reputation in practitioner discussions for being lightweight and fast, and
because a dedicated vector database seemed the natural component for the job.
In practice, it ran only in its embedded Milvus Lite mode, a single local
file. The configuration for a standalone Milvus server was written but never
used, because setting the server up proved more involved than the project
warranted.

\paragraph{Reversal.} In August 2025 the store was replaced by PostgreSQL~17
with the pgvector extension~\citep{pgvector}. PostgreSQL had not been on the
surveyed list at all, which is worth admitting: the survey had framed the
question as \emph{which vector database}, and an extension to a general
relational database fell outside that frame. The case for it rested on three
points. The project does not need a separate vector service, given a corpus of
a few thousand chunks. PostgreSQL was already familiar to the author. And
metadata filtering becomes plain SQL rather than a product-specific filter
language. A fourth benefit was not planned for. PostgreSQL ships with
language-aware full-text search, including a Portuguese configuration, so
when hybrid retrieval was added a year later (Section~\ref{subsec:search}),
its lexical half needed no new component.

\paragraph{Schema.} Each chunk is one row holding its text, the parent
section text, party and ideology, topic labels as an array, the heading path
as JSON, and a 1{,}024-dimensional vector. Similarity search uses an HNSW
index~\citep{malkov2020hnsw} on cosine distance
($m = 16$, $\mathit{ef\_construction} = 64$). Topic filters use a GIN index
on the label array. A generated \texttt{tsvector} column, computed with
PostgreSQL's Portuguese text-search configuration and also GIN-indexed,
serves lexical search. Because the column is generated by the database, it
cannot drift out of sync with the text it indexes. PostgreSQL runs in Docker,
so the whole store is reproducible from one configuration file.

Indexing happens once, ahead of time; retrieval happens every time a user asks
a question. That split between an offline and an online half is the usual
shape of a RAG system, and it explains several design choices in this chapter.
Offline work can be slow, expensive and strict. Indexing stops on the first
classification error because a bad label would be stored permanently
(Section~\ref{subsec:classification}). Online work must answer within seconds,
and a degraded answer is better than none, so every online step falls back to
a simpler behaviour when an LLM call fails.

Retrieval turns a question into a ranked set of manifesto passages in five
steps (Figure~\ref{fig:retrieval}). The question is analysed to find which
parties and topics it concerns. It is expanded into several reformulations,
routed to a search strategy, searched, and the resulting ranked lists are fused
into one. Every technique beyond plain dense search sits behind a feature flag
and can be switched off on its own. This is what allows the evaluation
(Section~\ref{sec:evaluation}) to measure each technique's contribution
separately. Most of the techniques come from the \textit{RAG from Scratch}
series~\citep{rag-from-scratch}; others were adapted from practitioner
tutorials and blog posts.

    flag in brackets; with the flag off, a stage falls back to its simplest
    form (Section~\ref{subsec:variants}). Reranking and the topic filter are
    off by default. A question that names only parties outside the corpus

\subsection{Query analysis} \label{subsec:queryanalysis}

Every chunk in the store carries its party and its topic labels. Those fields
were added with filtering in mind: if the system knows a question concerns
one party, or one policy area, it can search only the passages that match
and leave the rest of the corpus out. The first versions of the system
supported this only by hand. The caller passed the party or topic as an
explicit filter. Query analysis, introduced in March 2026, automates that
step: an LLM reads the question and extracts the filters itself, following
the logical-routing and metadata-filtering patterns of \textit{RAG from
Scratch}~\citep{rag-from-scratch}.

The analysis is a single structured-output call that returns six fields:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{parties}: the parties the question concerns. When a question
    refers to a bloc rather than naming parties (``os partidos de direita'',
    \textit{the right-wing parties}), the model lists the members of that bloc.
    The left--right placement it uses is stated in the prompt, following the
    Chapel Hill Expert Survey~\citep{jolly2022ches}, rather than left to the
    model's own view of Portuguese politics.
    \item \textbf{coverage}: whether the answer must represent every party in
    scope (\textit{exhaustive}) or the most relevant passages are enough,
    wherever they come from (\textit{best match}).
    \item \textbf{topics}: labels from the same 18-category taxonomy used at
    indexing time.
    \item \textbf{answer shape}: whether the answer should be laid out party by
    party or as a single text (Section~\ref{sec:generation}).
    \item \textbf{reformulated query}: a cleaned-up version of the question for
    search.
    \item \textbf{unlisted parties}: parties the question names that are not in
    the corpus.
\end{itemize}

version asked the model for a \emph{query type} directly: single party,
comparison, topic exploration or general. That conflated two independent
questions, namely which manifestos must be read and what the answer should
look like, and it failed on a whole class of questions. ``O que pensa cada
partido sobre a educação pública?'' (\textit{What does each party think about
public education?}) names no party and contains no comparison verb, so the model
labelled it a topic exploration. That route runs one search across the whole
corpus and keeps the top results, so the answer covered whichever parties
happened to rank highest. Nothing in the output showed the others had been
dropped.

Since August 2026 the model only describes the question, through the fields
above, and the query type is derived from them by a fixed rule. Two or more
parties, or exhaustive coverage, yield a comparison. One party yields a
single-party query. Otherwise, topics yield a topic exploration, and the rest
is general. There is now one source of truth: the router cannot disagree with
the analyser, and the analyser's output can be scored against hand-written
annotations without depending on any particular route. The same revision
removed an \emph{ideology} field. A single left--right label is too coarse
for questions about, say, ``the conservative parties'', so bloc language is now
resolved into an explicit list of parties, which is visible and can be checked.
The revision also fixed party matching, which worked on substrings:
``PSD'' resolved to the Partido Socialista because ``ps'' is inside ``psd'', so
a question about one party was answered from another's manifesto, with
citations that looked correct. Names are now matched as whole words, and
coalition members resolve to the programme actually published (PSD, CDS-PP
and PPM to AD; the CDU's PCP and PEV to PCP).

\paragraph{Revision: parties outside the corpus.} Gold question q-59 asks what
ADN proposes for housing. ADN is a real party; its manifesto is not in the
corpus. Told to use only the names on its list, the analyser did the obliging
thing and mapped ADN to AD, and the answer presented AD's housing plans as
ADN's, with tidy citations. The sixth field fixes this. A question naming only
unlisted parties skips the search and gets a fixed answer that says so and
lists the eight parties covered. A mixed one (``PS e ADN'') is answered for
the corpus party, with a one-line note up front. A first live check still read
``PS e ADN'' as AD and sent PPM to PAN, so the party table the analyser sees
now spells out initials and coalition members. Why not keep a hand-made list
of parties outside the corpus? Because ``ADN'' is also Portuguese for DNA.

\paragraph{Fallback.} If the analysis call fails after its retries, a
deterministic fallback takes over. It extracts only what needs no
interpretation: party names, matched exactly against a list of aliases. Every
other field defaults towards wider coverage: no topic filter, and exhaustive
coverage so that the router searches each party separately. The search may be
wider than the question needed, but no party is silently left out.

\subsection{Multi-query expansion} \label{subsec:multiquery}

A question and the passage that answers it often use different words. A user
asks about \textit{rendas} (rents); the manifesto speaks of
\textit{arrendamento acessível} (affordable letting). Dense embeddings narrow
this gap but do not close it. Multi-query expansion~\citep{rag-from-scratch,
rackauckas2024ragfusion} asks the LLM for four reformulations of the
question, each with a different vocabulary or perspective, and searches with
all five versions. A passage missed by the original wording can then be found
by one of the reformulations. The reformulations are fused into a single
ranking (Section~\ref{subsec:search}), so this adds recall at the cost of one
extra LLM call and four extra searches per question.

\subsection{Routing and metadata filtering} \label{subsec:routing}

The derived query type selects one of four search strategies:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Single party}: search only that party's chunks, keeping the
    top ten.
    \item \textbf{Comparison}: search each party in scope separately, eight
    results per party, and keep the results grouped by party. When the question
    names no party, every party is in scope.
    \item \textbf{Topic exploration}: the same per-party search. ``What is
    proposed on X?'' is a comparison in disguise.
    \item \textbf{General}: search the whole corpus, keeping the top ten.
\end{itemize}

The comparison route matters most for a civic tool. A single search over the
whole corpus returns whichever passages score highest, and parties with
longer or more verbose manifestos win more of those slots. Searching each
party separately gives every party the same number of slots. It is the only
route that guarantees no party is crowded out of an answer that claims to
cover them all.

\paragraph{Revision: topics as a preference.} Topic filters were originally
strict: a search for health-related passages returned only chunks labelled
\textit{saude}, or nothing. In August 2026 a question the corpus could answer
well came back with ``não foram encontrados documentos relevantes''
(\textit{no relevant documents were found}). The analyser had proposed the
topic \textit{saude}, but that index had been built without classification, so
every chunk carried the default label and the intersection was empty. The same
failure occurs whenever the analyser proposes a topic the classifier never
assigned. Topics are a precision aid, not a correctness requirement, so they
became a preference: the search runs with the topic filter first and, if
nothing matches, runs again without it and logs a warning. Comparison queries
relax the filter per party, so a party that does not mention a topic cannot
remove it from the search for the others.

\paragraph{Revision: no filter at all.} The gold set then showed the filter
hurting even when it matched something. On the development questions, 41\% of
the gold chunks the system missed had been excluded by the topic filter, often
because the analyser answered \textit{outros}, the catch-all, which as a filter
keeps only the chunks the classifier could not place. A topic exploration also
got a single search of five chunks for eight parties, so a question could score
zero with every relevant chunk sitting in its party's top three. Topic
explorations now fan out per party, and the filter is off by default. Together
with a wider net (10 chunks, 8 per party, up from 5 and 5), this was judged on
all 61 gold questions: recall +0.123 (CI [+0.08, +0.17]) and correctness
+0.041 (CI [+0.01, +0.07]), with faithfulness unchanged within noise. The
filter survives as an ablation (Section~\ref{subsec:variants}); topics still
decide whether a question is a topic exploration.

\subsection{Search and fusion} \label{subsec:search}

Each query version is embedded and matched against the stored chunk vectors
by cosine similarity, within whatever party filter the route applies. Multi-query expansion produces one ranked list per version, and these
are merged with Reciprocal Rank Fusion (RRF)~\citep{cormack2009rrf}. RRF
scores each chunk by the sum of $1/(k + r)$ over the lists in which it
appears at rank $r$, with $k = 60$. It uses only ranks, not scores, so lists
whose scores are on different scales can be fused without calibration, and a
chunk that ranks well for several reformulations rises above one that ranks
first for only one.

\paragraph{Hybrid retrieval.} Dense embeddings capture meaning but can
under-weight rare exact strings: acronyms, programme names, codes
(\textit{RES\_U}, \textit{TMRG}, \textit{USF tipo C}). These are often exactly
what a user quotes. Hybrid retrieval adds a lexical search over PostgreSQL's
Portuguese full-text index (Section~\ref{subsec:vectorstore}) for every query
version, and fuses all dense and lexical lists in a single RRF pass, so each
list carries the same weight.

The history of this feature is the second silent failure in this work, after
the encoder truncation of Section~\ref{subsec:chunking}. When hybrid retrieval
was added in August 2026, a handful of probes showed a smaller gain than
expected. Only one acronym was clearly rescued: \textit{RES\_U} went from zero
to two relevant chunks in the top five. In October 2026 the cause became
clear. The lexical search required \emph{every} word of the question to
appear in a chunk, so a natural-language question matched almost nothing. ``Qual
é a taxa única de IRS proposta pela Iniciativa Liberal?'' (\textit{What flat
IRS rate does the Iniciativa Liberal propose?}) matched none of IL's chunks.
The hybrid configuration had been running as dense search alone, with no error
to show it. Lexical search was rewritten as Okapi
BM25~\citep{robertson2009bm25}, in which any query term can match and rarer
terms weigh more. After the rewrite, lexical search alone found a relevant
chunk in its top ten for 44 of the 51 gold questions. Measured properly this
time, hybrid beat dense search alone on the development set (nDCG +0.064, with
a paired 95\% CI excluding zero; MRR +0.10) at no cost in latency, helping most
where a comparison's wording is exact. It has been the default since October
2026.

Two refinements followed. Accents are folded after stemming, so a question
typed without accents (``saude'') still finds chunks about \textit{saúde}.
Folding before stemming would break the Portuguese stemmer, which needs the
accents to recognise suffixes. And under a party filter, the party's name is
removed from the lexical query. As a rare term it would otherwise outweigh the
topic, even though the filter already guarantees every candidate belongs to
that party.

\paragraph{Party names in dense queries.} The same effect appeared in dense
search. On ``Qual é a posição do PAN sobre as touradas?'' (\textit{What is
PAN's position on bullfighting?}), searched within PAN's chunks, the best
relevant chunk ranked sixth; with ``PAN'' removed from the query, first. The
name pulled the query towards chunks \emph{about the party} (``O PAN é um
partido de causas\ldots''), which the filter already makes redundant. Stripping
names was built as a variant, but never needed a run: contextual retrieval
(Section~\ref{subsec:contextual}) already puts the gold chunks for that
question at ranks one to three.

\subsection{Reranking} \label{subsec:reranking}

Dense retrieval embeds the question and each chunk independently. That is what
makes searching a whole corpus affordable, and also what limits its precision:
a chunk's vector cannot depend on the question being asked. A
\emph{cross-encoder} reads the question and a passage together and scores
their relevance directly~\citep{nogueira2019passagererankingbert}. It is
far more accurate but far too slow to apply to the whole corpus, so it runs
second, over a shortlist.

With reranking enabled, each search fetches 30 candidates instead of the
usual ten, the fused list keeps all 30, and the cross-encoder
(\texttt{bge-reranker-v2-m3}~\citep{bge-reranker-v2-m3}, a multilingual
model) reorders them before the list is cut back. The wider fetch is the
point: a reranker can only promote what retrieval already found, so reranking
exactly ten candidates would reorder the answer without ever improving its
contents. The number of candidates is therefore the real limit on what
reranking can recall. The model scores the user's original question against
each \emph{small} chunk, not its parent section. Under small-to-big retrieval
many chunks share one parent, and scoring the parent would give all of them the
same score.

\paragraph{Verdict: it lost.} On the development set the reranker cut recall by
0.055 and doubled latency, so it stays off and is reported as a negative
result. That runs against the published experience, where reranking on top of
contextual retrieval cut failed retrievals further, from 49\% to 67\%
fewer~\citep{anthropic2024contextual}.

\subsection{Grounding and neutrality} \label{subsec:grounding}

All prompts are written in European Portuguese, and so are all answers. The
system prompt sets the rules the answer must follow:
\begin{itemize}
    \item answer \emph{only} from the context supplied, never from the model's
    own knowledge;
    \item cite the source of every claim (Section~\ref{subsec:citations});
    \item if the context does not contain the answer, reply with one fixed
    sentence, ``Não encontrei resposta a esta pergunta nos excertos dos
    programas que consultei, mas isso não garante que os programas não abordem
    o tema.'' (\textit{I found no answer to this question in the manifesto
    excerpts I consulted, but that does not mean the manifestos do not address
    it.}) The sentence reports what retrieval found, not what the manifestos
    hold: a top-$k$ search cannot prove that a manifesto never says something;
    \item describe the measures in neutral language, and attribute every
    justification, diagnosis or value judgement to the party that makes it
    (``o partido considera que\ldots''), never adopting the manifesto's
    evaluative terms as the system's own; leave out attacks on other parties
    unless the question asks for them;
    \item in comparisons, say explicitly when a party's manifesto does not
    address the topic.
\end{itemize}
Query analysis, expansion and generation all run at temperature zero. The same
question should produce the same answer, and a rerun should repeat, so a
difference between two runs is the change being measured rather than noise.

Questions about parties outside the corpus get a different fixed reply
(Section~\ref{subsec:queryanalysis}). The two must not be confused: one means
``I searched and found nothing'', the other ``I have nothing to search''.

The fixed refusal sentence has a practical purpose too. The evaluation detects
it to measure whether the system abstains on questions the manifestos cannot
answer, and whether it wrongly refuses questions they can
(Section~\ref{subsec:metrics}).

\paragraph{Revision: partial answers and false premises.} The first prompts
allowed only two outcomes: a full answer or a refusal. Real questions often
fall in between. The context covers part of the question, or the question
assumes something the manifestos do not say. Since October 2026 the prompts
ask the model to answer the part the context supports and to say what the
manifestos do not cover. When a question rests on a premise the context does
not confirm, the model should say so rather than accept it.

\subsection{Synthesis strategies} \label{subsec:synthesis}

The strategy is chosen by the \emph{answer shape} set during query analysis,
not by the retrieval route, because the two can differ. ``Que partido propõe
mais sobre habitação?'' (\textit{Which party proposes most on housing?}) must
retrieve every party, which is the comparison route, but wants a single
verdict, not eight sections. Two strategies follow:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Direct}: one prompt with all passages, producing one text.
    Used for narrative answers.
    \item \textbf{Comparison}: passages grouped under party headings, with a
    prompt asking for each party's position in turn and then the similarities
    and differences. Used for party-by-party answers, but only when at least two
    parties have results; a ``comparison'' of one party once closed with a
    solemn summary that there was nothing to compare.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Revision: decomposition, retired.} A third strategy, question
decomposition~\citep{rag-from-scratch}, split a question into sub-questions
and answered each from the same context. It was first triggered by any
question with more than eight passages, on the theory that big contexts needed
breaking down. They did not: decomposition reused the full context every time,
so it multiplied LLM calls (five per answer) without ever shrinking a prompt.
It was narrowed in August 2026 and retired in October: grouping the passages by
party and using the comparison prompt gives the same layout in one call.

\subsection{Fitting the context} \label{subsec:context}

Retrieval returns small chunks, but under small-to-big retrieval the generator
reads each chunk's parent section (Section~\ref{subsec:chunking}). Several
chunks often share a parent, so passages are deduplicated by the text the
generator will read. Otherwise one section could appear two or three times in
the same prompt.

The prompt has a budget of 32{,}000 tokens of context. The generation model
runs remotely, so its tokenizer is not available locally, and the budget is
enforced with a deliberately cautious estimate of 3.5 characters per token: it
overestimates, so trimming happens a little early rather than one passage too
late. When the context is too long, a flat list loses its lowest-ranked
passages. A context grouped by party is trimmed differently: passages come off
whichever party currently contributes most, and every party keeps at least one,
even if the prompt then exceeds the budget. An over-long prompt is a visible
failure; an answer that silently omits a party is not.

\paragraph{Revision: how big a budget.} The budget doubled twice. At 8{,}000
tokens an eight-party comparison overflowed with one passage per party. At
16{,}000, one all-party question (q-33, minimum wage) lost 11 of its 24
passages, 4 of them gold, and scored 0.43 on correctness; at 32{,}000 nothing
was trimmed and it scored 0.77. One question justifies a setting, not a
headline. 64{,}000 was then judged on all 61 gold questions and read no better
(correctness +0.029, within noise) for 18\% more input tokens, so the budget
stays at 32{,}000. Deduplication also grew smarter: overlapping parent windows
of the same section are now merged, not only identical ones (one tax question
went from five passages to three, about 5.3k tokens to 2.9k).

\paragraph{Revision: no party lost on the way to the answer.} The per-party
route of Section~\ref{subsec:routing} guarantees every party is
\emph{retrieved}. Two later defects showed that this does not guarantee every
party reaches the \emph{answer}.
\begin{itemize}
    \item A narrative answer over a per-party search read the router's flat
    list, then cut to the top five passages. A verdict claiming to weigh
    eight parties was written from five passages, with nothing in the output to
    show it. It now reads the full grouped set (August 2026).
    \item That grouped set was then trimmed to budget as one flat list, which
    could drop every passage of a low-ranking party. It is now trimmed per party
    before being flattened (October 2026).
\end{itemize}
In the same vein, a per-party search in which every party came back empty now
returns the fixed no-information answer instead of prompting the model with no
context. An answer that stops at the length limit is flagged as cut off,
instead of passing as complete.

\subsection{Citations} \label{subsec:citations}

Source transparency is a requirement, not a feature, for a system that
summarises political positions. Every passage in the prompt is numbered,
\texttt{[Documento 1]} to \texttt{[Documento $n$]}, across the whole
context, with its party and section path. The model must cite each claim
inline with the matching number, for example \texttt{[2]} or
\texttt{[1][3]}. Since the answer's list of sources uses the same numbers,
every claim can be traced to the chunk, section and manifesto it came from,
and the evaluation can check each claim against its cited passage
(Section~\ref{subsec:metrics}). Earlier prompts asked the model to mention the party
and section in prose, which named where information came from but could not
tie a statement to one specific passage.

A comparison between variants is only as good as the numbers on both sides,
and several failures in this pipeline would not raise an error: they would
quietly change a score. Each of the following is therefore checked
automatically, either by the evaluation harness itself or by a test in the
repository's test suite.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Consistent gold labels.} A gold question's route label is
    derived from its parties and coverage, exactly as the pipeline derives it.
    A question whose labels contradict each other is rejected when the gold set
    is loaded, instead of scoring zero on every variant.
    \item \textbf{Failures are errors, not scores.} The harness runs query
    analysis and query expansion in strict mode: a call that still fails after
    its retries is recorded as an error for that question, rather than falling
    back to a simpler pipeline whose output would be scored under the variant's
    name. A judge call that fails leaves its score out instead of recording a
    zero, so a free-tier outage cannot pass for a bad answer.
    \item \textbf{Exact filtered search.} pgvector applies metadata filters
    after the approximate (HNSW) index scan, so a filtered query can return
    fewer rows than requested; measured here, a party filter under an HNSW plan
    returned 3 of 30 requested rows \TODO{cite the pgvector documentation on
    filtering}. A test on a table shaped like the real one (same per-party
    sizes and vector dimension) checks that a party-filtered search returns the
    full top-$k$, so retrieval would not be silently starved if the query plan
    changed as the corpus grows.
    \item \textbf{Stable gold identity.} Gold chunks are identified by a hash of
    their content (Section~\ref{subsec:goldset}). A test checks that the
    contextual-header ablation changes only the text that is embedded, not the
    stored content, so one gold set scores every variant.
    \item \textbf{No silent substitutions.} A reranker that fails to load stops
    the run instead of returning un-reranked results under the
    \texttt{reranked} name. An answer cut off at the output token limit is
    marked in the per-question record, and the leaderboard notes any variant in
    which a fallback model answered some of the calls.
\end{itemize}
