\chapter{Results} \label{ch:results}

This chapter answers the five research questions of
Section~\ref{sec:research-questions} with the numbers the evaluation of
Section~\ref{sec:evaluation} produced. All of them come from one run per
variant on the 36 test questions (32 answerable and 4 not), made on the frozen
code of commit \texttt{0293a0b} after the last change to the system
(Section~\ref{subsec:safeguards}). Each figure is a mean over questions with a
95\% bootstrap interval, and each comparison between two variants is a paired
difference per question, marked with an asterisk when its interval excludes
zero.

The chapter starts with the answers \sysname{} gives (RQ3), because that is
what a reader of the manifestos meets. It then sets \sysname{} against models
without retrieval and against commercial assistants (RQ5), takes the ladder
apart one technique at a time (RQ1 and RQ2), and prices each step (RQ4). It
ends with the system in use, on questions outside the gold set.

\section{Setup} \label{sec:results-setup}

The method is the one of Section~\ref{sec:evaluation}, and only two points
need repeating. Every generation score comes from the Qwen judge, which was
checked claim by claim against the author's blind ratings
(Section~\ref{subsec:metrics}). Its correctness recall agrees with the ratings
at a Cohen's $\kappa$ of 0.90, and on a sample built to contain many
unsupported claims it caught 89\% of them, so the generation scores below can be read as
measurements. When judge and rater disagree the judge is usually the stricter,
so the scores lean pessimistic. Its relevance score, on the other hand, tells
whether an answer is on topic and not whether it is right, since it gave the
top mark to closed-book answers that got almost nothing right. Relevance is
therefore not reported as a quality score. It is used only through its
\emph{abstained} flag, which gives abstention on the unanswerable questions
and false refusal on the answerable ones.

Correctness needs a word of caution too. It is an F1 over claims, and its
precision half counts as wrong any claim the reference answer does not
contain, true or not. Asked how much the PCP wants to raise the minimum wage
(q-02), ChatGPT answered ``1\,000 euros mensais em 2024''\footnote{In English, ``1,000
euros a month in 2024''.}, which is right, and scored zero precision because
the reference does not say ``mensais''. Long answers that bring in outside detail lose the most, which
matters when \sysname{} meets the assistants. Comparisons across systems are
therefore led by \emph{correctness recall}, the share of the reference the
answer covers, with the F1 reported beside it.

Full tables for every variant and metric are in Appendix~\ref{app:results}.
They average each variant over the questions every variant was scored on,
while the tables in this chapter average each variant over its own scored
questions, so the two can differ by up to a hundredth.

\paragraph{Retrieval.} The gold chunks are mostly found. On the answerable
questions \sysname{} hands the generator 87\% of the chunks a human picked as
the answer, and at least one of them every time (hit rate 1.00). The eight
broad questions, those with more gold chunks than the system has slots, are
harder, and there it finds 85\% of what fits (capped recall 0.847) and draws on
77\% of the manifesto sections involved (aspect recall 0.769).

\paragraph{Grounding.} The answers stick to their sources. Faithfulness is
0.952, and the judge validation found the same at the claim level, with 99\%
of claims supported by the excerpts. The one low score in the table, 0.750 on
general questions, is a single answer (q-49) that refused to answer and so
had no supported claim to count.

\paragraph{Coverage.} Correct is not the same as complete. An answer covers
on average 63\% of its reference, well below its faithfulness. The answers say
true things from the manifestos and miss others. Where they fall shortest is
telling. Topic explorations (``which parties talk about X?'') and general
questions ask about every party at once, their references list many aspects,
and an answer covers only 30 to 44\% of them. Single-party questions and
comparisons of two or three parties fare best, at 74 and 71\%.

\paragraph{Refusals.} \sysname{} declined all four unanswerable questions,
and wrongly declined two of the 32 answerable ones. Both false refusals had
the evidence in hand. In q-49 the PCP's account of restoring the 35-hour week
in 2016 was among the excerpts, and in q-56 the CHEGA's recruitment
proposals were too. These are failures of generation, not of retrieval. With
four unanswerable questions, a single miss would move abstention by 0.25, so
these rates are reported as counts.

\section{Retrieval versus scale} \label{sec:results-scale}

RQ5 asks whether a small model that reads the manifestos can match a larger
one that does not. Table~\ref{tab:results-scale} sets \texttt{current} against
three groups. The first answers from memory, either the same Gemma model
(\texttt{no\_retrieval}) or GPT-5.4 (\texttt{no\_retrieval\_strong}). The second
is GPT-5.4 with \sysname{}'s retrieval (\texttt{current\_strong}). The third is
what a citizen can use today, which is ChatGPT, Gemini and Claude with web
search on (\texttt{assistant\_*}), plus a research agent with no limits
(\texttt{deep\_research\_claude}). That agent is Claude Opus 5.5 run through Claude
Code in an empty folder, with only web search and page fetching enabled, at
high reasoning effort and with no cap on searches, turns or tokens. It
answered with the same instructions as the assistants, after 6.1 searches and
8.6 pages read per question on average. It ran on a subscription, so its cost
is the one Claude Code reports, a notional figure rather than money spent.

\paragraph{Memory is not enough.} A model answering from memory covers 9\% of
the reference, and GPT-5.4, a frontier model, covers 13\%. Scale helps a
little, since the difference between the two is significant (+0.042*), but
both lose to \sysname{} by half the reference. Manifesto detail, the specific
figure or the name of a programme, is not in the weights of either model. The
two also differ in how they handle what they do not know. Asked what the PCP
proposes on legalising cannabis, which its manifesto never mentions, the small
model listed proposals, while GPT-5.4 said it knew of none. The judge counts an
answer as declined only when it refuses outright, so GPT-5.4 is credited with
one refusal of four even though two more of its answers hedge and a third
rejects the question's false premise.

\paragraph{Scale on top of retrieval helps.} Given the same excerpts, GPT-5.4
covers 75\% of the reference against Gemma's 63\% (+0.111*). It is the only
variant that beats \sysname{} on correctness recall with an interval clear of
zero. It does so at 37 times the cost per answer and more than twice the
median latency, and three of its answers ran into the 3\,000-token cap.

\paragraph{The assistants do not beat \sysname{}.} ChatGPT and Gemini with web
search cover 55 to 56\% of the reference, about eight points below
\sysname{}, with intervals that cross zero. On that measure they are no better,
at 60 to 85 times the cost and four to nine times the median latency.
Gemini's F1 is significantly lower ($-$0.111*), because its answers add much
that the references do not contain.\footnote{The Gemini run hit provider
errors (rate limits, then timeouts) on 10 of the 36 questions. Those questions
were retried twice on the same code, and the reported figures come from the
complete file. The incomplete files score correctness recall 0.585 and 0.572
and abstention 0.50 and 0.67, but over 24 and 26 answered questions and over
2 and 3 of the 4 unanswerable ones, so the spread reflects which questions
were missing and not run-to-run variation. Its mean latency, 91\,s, is
inflated by the retries, which is why the median is reported.} The Claude
assistant trails clearly ($-$0.283*). Its answers often say that it could not
reach the manifesto and fall back on press coverage and parliamentary
records, which is honest behaviour but answers a different question. Its
answers were also the longest of the three assistants, and five of them ran
into the 3\,000-token cap.

\paragraph{A research agent can match it, slowly.} The unlimited research
agent covers 63\% of the reference, the same as \sysname{} ($-$0.005). It needs
108 seconds on average and a notional \$0.35 per answer, more than 300 times
\sysname{}'s cost, and its F1 is far lower (0.295) because its long answers
carry much that the references leave out. It is credited with declining two of the four
unanswerable questions, but its other two answers say, in prose, that the
manifesto has no such proposal, which the judge does not count as declining.
Abstention therefore compares \sysname{}, whose refusal is a fixed sentence,
with the other systems only loosely.

\paragraph{Retrieval (RQ2).} Two techniques carry the gains. Query analysis
with routing, which searches each party's manifesto separately when a
question names several, adds 21 points of recall and 11 of correctness
recall on its own. Lexical search adds another 12 points of recall and 10 of
correctness recall, because the words of a question (``propinas'', ``IRS
Jovem'') often appear verbatim in the passage that answers it, and a dense
encoder can rank those passages below vaguer ones on the same theme. Multi-query
expansion did nothing measurable, neither on the naive pipeline nor after
routing. It remains in the production pipeline, but the test split gives no
reason to credit it with any of the gains. Taken
together, the ladder doubles recall (0.452 to 0.870) and raises correctness
recall from 0.41 to 0.63.

\paragraph{Representation (RQ1).} Writing the section path into each chunk
before embedding it changed nothing measurable. Adding the LLM-written context
of Section~\ref{subsec:contextual} ranked the gold chunks higher (nDCG
+0.040*), and against the plain index it found more of them (recall +0.043*,
from \texttt{hybrid} to \texttt{current}), but the answers did not improve
(correctness recall +0.008). On all 61 questions, before the split was
fixed, the same step had looked larger (recall +0.046), and the test split
shrinks it to a gain in
ranking that the generator, which reads every excerpt it is given whatever
its rank, does not seem to turn into better answers. The larger representation finding came before the
ladder. Measuring chunks in characters had left 62.7\% of the corpus
unembedded under the Serafim encoder (Section~\ref{subsec:chunking}), and no
rung would have been worth measuring without that fix.

\paragraph{Reranking, a negative result.} A cross-encoder that re-scores 30
candidates and keeps the best ten is the textbook way to improve
precision~\citep{nogueira2019passagererankingbert}. Here it put the first gold chunk
slightly higher (MRR +0.030, not significant) but lost 11 points of recall
($-$0.111*), and it more than doubled the latency. The same loss appeared on the
development questions ($-$0.055). On the naive pipeline the same reranker helps
(recall +0.062*, nDCG +0.113*), so the problem is not the model. A likely reason is
that the cross-encoder reads each chunk bare, without the section path and
the LLM-written context that help \texttt{current} rank it, so once fusion
already orders the candidates well it has more to lose than to gain.

\paragraph{Routing scores.} The scores of the query analysis itself (query
type, coverage, parties and topics) are in Table~\ref{tab:app-routing}. They
are the analyser's and not a rung's, since every rung from
\texttt{dense\_only} up shares one analysis per question. One caveat follows
from that table. The \texttt{routed} rung ran in a separate process and drew a
different analysis on 6 of the 36 questions, because the analyser is not
stable even at temperature zero. Its slightly higher routing scores are that
draw, not a better router, and its recall is still 21 points below
\texttt{current}'s.

\paragraph{The ladder is nearly free.} \sysname{} answers for about a tenth
of a cent, \$1.10 per thousand answers, and the whole ladder adds 30 cents per
thousand answers to the naive pipeline's cost and about three seconds of
latency. Routing doubles the tokens the generator reads, and those are still
the best-spent tokens in the system.
Every technique the ladder kept is worth its price at this scale. The one
that is not is the reranker, which more than doubles latency for a loss.

\paragraph{The alternatives are not.} The one variant that beats \sysname{}
on coverage, the frontier model with the same retrieval, costs 37 times as
much for 11 more points. For a tool paid for by a university project, or
offered free to voters, that trade does not pay. The assistants cost 60 to 85
times as much and are no better, and the research agent costs over 300 times
as much to match it.

\paragraph{Judging costs more than answering.} Scoring one \texttt{current}
answer costs \$0.0084, almost eight times the answer itself, because the judge
reads the excerpts and the reference claim by claim. Apart from the
assistants, evaluation and not the system is where the money of this work
went.

\paragraph{The answer.} The answer, in full, cites a document for every
point. The rates come from the two sections on tax reform (Documento 1 and 2),
the aim from the section on the demographic crisis (Documento 4), and the
year from the AD's budget scenario (Documento 3).

\begin{Verbatim}[fontsize=\scriptsize, breaklines=true, frame=single]
A Aliança Democrática (AD) propõe as seguintes medidas relativas ao **IRS Jovem**:

*   **Adoção de forma duradoura e estrutural**, o que implica uma redução de 2/3 nas taxas atualmente aplicáveis [1].
*   Aplicação de uma **taxa máxima de apenas 15%** a todos os jovens até aos 35 anos, com exceção do último escalão de rendimentos [1][2].
*   O objetivo da medida é reforçar os rendimentos dos jovens até aos 35 anos e reconhecer a perda gerada pela emigração massiva [4].
*   No plano orçamental, a adoção do IRS Jovem está prevista para o ano de **2025** [3].
\end{Verbatim}

\paragraph{The scores.} All seven claims in the answer are in the excerpts,
so faithfulness is 1.00. The reference, written by hand from the gold chunks,
makes seven claims, and the answer covers six of them (correctness recall
0.857). The one it misses is the aim. The reference says the AD wants to
``contrariar a emigração'',\footnote{``Counter emigration''.} while the answer
kept the manifesto's own words, ``reconhecer a perda gerada pela emigração
massiva'',\footnote{``Acknowledge the loss caused by mass emigration''.} and the
judge read these as different claims. Precision loses the same aim from the
other side, and also the year 2025, which the answer took correctly from the
budget table but the reference never mentions. Correctness ends at 0.779 for
an answer a reader would call right. This is the precision caveat of
Section~\ref{sec:results-setup}, seen on a single question, and it is why
correctness recall leads the comparisons.

\subsection{Where it stumbles} \label{subsec:results-stumbles}

\paragraph{Follow-up questions.} The last two questions were asked one after
the other, as a person would. ``Quais são os planos da AD para a
habitação?''\footnote{``What are the AD's plans for housing?''} got a normal
answer from the AD's housing chapter, listing six sources. ``E o PS?''\footnote{``And
the PS?''} got this.

\begin{Verbatim}[fontsize=\scriptsize, breaklines=true, frame=single]
[...]
[  4.6s] A pesquisar no programa de Partido Socialista…
[  5.4s] A redigir a resposta a partir de 10 excertos…

Não encontrei resposta a esta pergunta nos excertos dos programas que consultei, mas isso não garante que os programas não abordem o tema.

Fontes (7):
  [1] Partido Socialista – PLANO DE AÇÃO PARA PORTUGAL INTEIRO 2024 > INTRODUÇÃO > **2.ª MISSÃO:** UM ESTADO SOCIAL FORTE, MODERNO E INCLUSIVO > [...]
  [2] Partido Socialista – PLANO DE AÇÃO PARA PORTUGAL INTEIRO 2024 > INTRODUÇÃO (rrf=0.0763)
  [...]
\end{Verbatim}

Each call to \sysname{} starts from nothing. The question names a party and
no topic, so routing searched the PS manifesto with nothing to look for, and
the search returned its introduction. The PS manifesto has a housing chapter (``Uma
habitação digna para todos''), and that is what the person meant. The refusal is at least honest about what happened,
but it is the wrong outcome, and the evaluation never sees it, since every
gold question stands alone (Chapter~\ref{ch:conclusion}).

\paragraph{Refusing with the answer in hand.} The evaluation found the
opposite failure as well. Asked in what year the 35-hour week was restored in
the public administration (q-49), \sysname{} retrieved the PCP passage that
gives the year, 2016, and still answered that it found nothing. The same
happened with the CHEGA's recruitment proposals (q-56). Both are the two false
refusals of Table~\ref{tab:results-card}, and both are the generator being
too cautious, not the search missing.

\paragraph{Wrong absence claims.} The reverse also happens, if rarely. In
q-51 an answer stated that the PAN does not mention euthanasia, while one of
its own excerpts was the PAN calling for the law to be regulated. The judge
caught it, and faithfulness dropped for that answer, but a reader would have
been told something false about a party.

\paragraph{The budget.} The animal welfare question retrieved 64 excerpts, 8
per party, and the prompt budget of Section~\ref{subsec:context} dropped 8 of
them. Every party kept a section, so the answer stayed balanced, but the
lowest-ranked excerpts were never read. On questions about every party, the
budget as well as the search limits how much of each manifesto the answer can
reflect, which may explain part of the low coverage of topic explorations in
Table~\ref{tab:results-card}.

\section{Summary} \label{sec:results-summary}

\paragraph{RQ1 (Representation).} Section headers changed nothing, and the
LLM-written context improved ranking (nDCG +0.040*) without improving the
answers. The representation choice that mattered was sizing chunks in the
encoder's own tokens, without which most of the corpus was never embedded.

\paragraph{RQ2 (Retrieval).} Routing per party and hybrid lexical search carry
the gains, together doubling recall over the naive pipeline. Multi-query
expansion adds nothing measurable, and cross-encoder reranking loses recall
($-$0.111*).

\paragraph{RQ3 (Generation).} Better retrieval gives better answers, with
correctness recall rising from 0.409 to 0.634. The answers are faithful
(0.952) but incomplete, and they are weakest on questions about every party at
once.

\paragraph{RQ4 (Cost).} \sysname{} answers for \$0.0011 in about five seconds, and the techniques it kept add little to either. The reranker
and the frontier model are the only steps whose cost the results do not
justify.

\paragraph{RQ5 (Retrieval versus scale).} A small model with the manifestos
covers five times as much of the reference as a frontier model without them,
matches the commercial assistants and an unlimited research agent at a
sixtieth to a three-hundredth of their cost, and declined every unanswerable
question. A frontier model with the same
retrieval does better still, at 37 times the price.

Chapter~\ref{ch:conclusion} turns these answers into conclusions, and sets
out the limits of what 36 questions and one rater can show.
