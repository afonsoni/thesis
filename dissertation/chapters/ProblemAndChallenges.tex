\section{Problem statement} \label{sec:problem-statement}

Party manifestos are the most complete public statement of what each party
proposes to do in government, and almost no one reads them. The eight
manifestos of the 2024 Portuguese legislative election run from 94 to 275
pages each, roughly 1{,}400 pages in all. They differ in structure,
vocabulary and level of detail, and they are written in European Portuguese, a
language with far fewer NLP resources than English. A voter who wants to know
what each party proposes on housing would have to find the relevant passages
in eight long documents and compare them on their own.

The problem this thesis studies is whether, and how, Retrieval-Augmented
Generation can make such documents genuinely accessible: answering a citizen's
question with what the manifestos actually say, attributed to the right party,
traceable to the passage it came from, and without favouring any party. The
system built for this, \sysname{} (Chapter~\ref{ch:proposition},
Figure~\ref{fig:system-overview}), is the instrument of the study, not its
goal. The research questions (Section~\ref{sec:research-questions}) ask which
of its techniques actually help, at what cost, and whether retrieval still
earns its place next to ever stronger language models.

The rest of this chapter sets out the challenges that made the problem hard
before any code was written. Some come from the domain, some from the setting
of an academic thesis, and some from the speed at which the field itself was
changing. Each challenge is followed by the approaches available and,
briefly, by the one chosen. The Proposition develops each choice in full,
including the cases where it was later revised.

\section{Challenges of the domain} \label{sec:challenges-domain}
% ============================================================

\subsection{European Portuguese as a low-resource language}
\label{subsec:challenge-language}

Most embedding models, rerankers and benchmarks are built for English. Where
Portuguese is covered, it is usually Brazilian Portuguese, which differs from
the European variant in vocabulary, spelling and political terminology. Models
built specifically for European Portuguese exist but are few, recent and less
tested. Portuguese also has features that make retrieval harder. Many common
words are homonyms: \textit{canto} is ``corner'' or ``I sing'', \textit{rio}
is ``river'' or ``I laugh''. Rich inflection means one concept appears in
many surface forms (\textit{habitação}, \textit{habitações},
\textit{habitacional}).

\paragraph{Possible approaches.} Use an encoder trained specifically for
European Portuguese; use a multilingual encoder trained on many languages
including Portuguese; or translate the corpus and the questions into English
and work with English models, at the cost of translation errors in a domain
where wording matters.

\paragraph{Chosen approach.} Work in Portuguese throughout: no translation,
prompts and answers in European Portuguese. The encoder was first a
Portuguese-native model and later a multilingual one with a far longer input
window. Whether a native encoder would do better on this corpus is measured
rather than assumed (Section~\ref{subsec:embeddings}).

\subsection{Documents written for print, not for machines}
\label{subsec:challenge-documents}

Manifestos are designed to be printed and read. They use multi-column layouts,
decorative title pages, running page headers, tables of contents and
infographics, and their heading structure exists only visually. A RAG system
needs clean running text in reading order and, ideally, the chapter and section
structure, because that structure says what each passage is about.

\paragraph{Possible approaches.} Plain-text PDF extractors, which are fast but
lose layout and headings; layout-aware parsers that output structured
Markdown; commercial cloud parsing services; and manual or LLM-assisted
cleaning of whatever the parser produces.

\paragraph{Chosen approach.} A local, layout-aware parser producing Markdown,
followed by cleaning: by hand for the first four manifestos, then with an
automated, verified cleaner reviewed by hand
(Sections~\ref{subsec:parsing} and~\ref{subsec:cleaning}).

\subsection{Political stakes: faithfulness and neutrality}
\label{subsec:challenge-stakes}

In most RAG applications a slightly wrong answer is an inconvenience. Here, an
answer that misstates a party's position is misinformation about an election.
Fluency is not enough: a confident, well-written answer that attributes a
proposal to the wrong party is worse than no answer at all. Language models
also carry political leanings of their own, absorbed from their training
data~\citep{pretraining-political-bias}. If left to fill gaps from memory, they
may colour an answer with them.

\paragraph{Possible approaches.} Constrain the model to the retrieved text
through its instructions; require citations so that every claim can be
checked; allow, and measure, explicit abstention when the manifestos do not
answer a question; and keep anything that encodes a political judgement
explicit and documented rather than implicit in a model.

\paragraph{Chosen approach.} All four. Answers come only from the retrieved
passages, each claim cites its passage, and a fixed sentence signals that the
manifestos do not answer the question (Section~\ref{sec:generation}). The one
place where the system must make a political judgement, deciding which parties
``the right-wing parties'' refers to, uses a stated and cited placement rather
than the model's own view (Section~\ref{subsec:queryanalysis}).

\subsection{Fairness between parties}
\label{subsec:challenge-fairness}

The manifestos differ greatly in length and style. A standard retrieval step
returns the passages most similar to a question, wherever they come from. That
favours parties that write more, or that happen to phrase things like the
question. For a question about one party this does not matter. For ``what does
each party propose on housing?'' it does: an answer that silently covers five
parties while appearing to cover eight is biased by omission, and nothing in
it would reveal the bias.

\paragraph{Possible approaches.} Retrieve a fixed share of passages per party;
re-balance results after retrieval; or report coverage alongside the answer so
gaps are at least visible.

\paragraph{Chosen approach.} When a question concerns several or all parties,
each party is searched separately and given the same number of slots, and the
answer stage is built not to drop a party once retrieved
(Sections~\ref{subsec:routing} and~\ref{subsec:context}).

\subsection{Many kinds of question}
\label{subsec:challenge-questions}

Citizens ask different kinds of question, and each needs different retrieval.
``What does PAN propose on bullfighting?'' concerns one party. ``How do PS and
AD differ on taxes?'' compares two. ``What does each party propose on
education?'' needs all eight. ``Which party proposes most on housing?'' needs
all eight but wants a single verdict. ``What do the right-wing parties say
about immigration?'' names a group, not parties. A single fixed pipeline serves
some of these well and the rest badly.

\paragraph{Possible approaches.} One general pipeline for everything;
classifying questions into types and routing each type to its own strategy;
decomposing complex questions into simpler sub-questions; or letting an
LLM agent plan the retrieval itself.

\paragraph{Chosen approach.} An LLM analyses each question to find the parties,
topics, coverage and answer layout it implies; a fixed rule turns that into a
route; and the answer strategy follows from the layout. Agentic retrieval is
planned as a separate variant, to be measured against these
(Sections~\ref{subsec:queryanalysis}, \ref{subsec:routing},
\ref{subsec:synthesis} and~\ref{subsec:agentic}).

\section{Challenges of the research setting}
\label{sec:challenges-setting}
% ============================================================

\subsection{No ground truth}
\label{subsec:challenge-groundtruth}

Evaluating a question-answering system needs questions with known answers.
No such dataset exists for Portuguese party manifestos. General RAG
benchmarks are in English and about other domains, and their results do not
transfer. Without ground truth, every design choice in this thesis would rest
on a handful of hand-picked examples, which is exactly how the failures
described in the Proposition went unnoticed for months.

\paragraph{Possible approaches.} Write questions and answers entirely by hand,
which is reliable but slow and small; generate them with an LLM from the
corpus, which is fast but risks testing the generator against itself; or a
hybrid in which generated candidates are checked and anchored to the source
passages by a human. Answer quality can then be judged by humans, by an LLM
acting as judge, or both.

\paragraph{Chosen approach.} The hybrid: candidate questions are drafted,
checked against the manifestos, and each is anchored to the passages that
answer it, with an LLM judge whose agreement with human judgement is itself
measured (Sections~\ref{subsec:goldset} and~\ref{subsec:metrics}).

\subsection{Limited resources}
\label{subsec:challenge-resources}

This is an academic project: no budget for paid APIs at scale, a laptop rather
than a GPU server, and free-tier services with strict rate limits. Indexing
eight manifestos requires hundreds of rate-limited LLM calls for topic
classification, and evaluating each variant requires hundreds more.

\paragraph{Possible approaches.} Run open models locally; use the free tiers
of hosted services; or pay for commercial APIs.

\paragraph{Chosen approach.} Small open models where they suffice (the
encoder and reranker run locally) and free-tier hosted LLMs for generation and
classification, with the code built around their limits: retries, fallback
models, and long jobs that can resume where they stopped
(Sections~\ref{subsec:classification} and~\ref{subsec:llm}). Keeping every
component free and open has a second benefit: anyone can run or audit the
system.

\section{A moving target} \label{sec:challenges-moving}
% ============================================================

This thesis was carried out between 2024 and 2026, while RAG and language
models changed faster than at any earlier time. That speed is a challenge in
its own right, and arguably the one most specific to this work.

\subsection{Unsettled best practices}
\label{subsec:challenge-practices}

When the work began there was no settled way to build a RAG system. How large
should chunks be, measured in what? Which encoder suits a non-English
retrieval task? Is keyword search still worth combining with embeddings? How
should a RAG system even be evaluated? Practitioner advice on each of these
changed within months, often without evidence, and new techniques appeared
roughly every other month. A choice that followed the best guidance available
could be outdated by the time it was implemented. Several were: the chunk-size
advice shifted more than once during the project, and the encoder chosen in
2025 had been overtaken by a new generation of retrieval-trained models
(Section~\ref{subsec:sota-recent}).

\paragraph{Possible approaches.} Freeze the design early and accept that it
will age; chase each new technique as it appears; or design so that change is
cheap and measurable.

\paragraph{Chosen approach.} The third. The system is modular, every technique
sits behind a feature flag, and a ladder of variants measures what each one
adds over the same questions (Section~\ref{subsec:variants}). Each decision,
and each reversal, is recorded with its reason. The Proposition presents
them as a history rather than as if the final design had been planned from the
start.

\subsection{Models that outpace the system}
\label{subsec:challenge-obsolescence}

The language models themselves improved even faster. The candidates surveyed
at the start of this work were overtaken within a year by models that were
better, cheaper and able to read far longer inputs
(Section~\ref{subsec:llm}). Each new release raised an uncomfortable question:
if a frontier model can read a whole manifesto in one prompt, or already
knows its contents, is a retrieval system still needed at all?

\paragraph{Possible approaches.} Ignore the question; assume that retrieval
helps; or treat it as a hypothesis and test it.

\paragraph{Chosen approach.} Test it. RQ5 compares \sysname{} with language
models answering without retrieval, on the same questions and judged the same
way (Section~\ref{sec:research-questions}). The arguments for retrieval in
this domain go beyond answer quality, to verifiability, neutrality,
long-tail knowledge and cost. They are discussed in
Section~\ref{subsec:sota-recent}, and revisited in light of the results in
Chapter~\ref{ch:conclusion}.
