\section{Problem statement} \label{sec:problem-statement}

Manifestos are the fullest public statement of what each party would do in
government, and almost nobody reads them. The eight from the 2024 Portuguese
legislative election run to roughly 1{,}400 pages between them, each with its
own structure, vocabulary and level of detail, all in European Portuguese. A
voter who wants to compare housing proposals has eight long documents and an
afternoon to lose.

This thesis asks whether Retrieval-Augmented Generation can close that gap:
answer a citizen's question with what the manifestos actually say, attributed
to the right party, traceable to its source, and fair to every party.
\sysname{} (Chapter~\ref{ch:proposition}) is the instrument for answering
that question, not the goal. The challenges below shaped it before a line of
code was written.

\section{Challenges of the domain} \label{sec:challenges-domain}
% ============================================================

\subsection{European Portuguese} \label{subsec:challenge-language}

Most encoders, rerankers and benchmarks are built for English. Where
Portuguese is covered, it is usually Brazilian. Portuguese-native models
exist but are few, and the language adds its own traps: homonyms
(\textit{canto} is ``corner'' or ``I sing''), and rich inflection that
scatters one concept across many word forms. The options were a native
encoder, a multilingual one, or translating everything into English and
working there. Translation was ruled out, since wording matters too much in
this domain. The encoder went from native to multilingual, for reasons that had more to
do with input length than with language (Section~\ref{subsec:embeddings}).

\subsection{Documents built for print} \label{subsec:challenge-documents}

Manifestos are designed to be read on paper: multiple columns, decorative
covers, running headers, infographics, and a heading structure that exists
only visually. Retrieval needs clean text in reading order, plus the section
structure that says what each passage is about. Plain-text extractors lose
the structure, and cloud parsers cost money. The choice was a local,
layout-aware parser that outputs Markdown, followed by cleaning: by hand at
first, later automated and checked (Sections~\ref{subsec:parsing}
and~\ref{subsec:cleaning}).

\subsection{Political stakes} \label{subsec:challenge-stakes}

Elsewhere, a slightly wrong answer is an inconvenience. Here it is
misinformation about an election, and a fluent wrong answer is worse than none.
Language models also bring political leanings of their
own~\citep{pretraining-political-bias}. The answer is to keep the model on a
short leash. It answers only from the retrieved text, cites every claim, and
has a fixed way to say ``the manifestos do not answer this''. The one
political judgement the system cannot avoid, which parties ``the right'' means,
comes from a cited source rather than the model's opinion
(Sections~\ref{subsec:queryanalysis} and~\ref{sec:generation}).

\subsection{Fairness between parties} \label{subsec:challenge-fairness}

Standard retrieval returns the passages that score highest, wherever they come
from, which rewards the parties that write the most. For ``what does each
party propose on housing?'', an answer that quietly covers five parties while
appearing to cover eight is bias by omission. So questions about several
parties search each party separately, with equal slots, and the answer stage
is built not to drop a party once it has been found
(Sections~\ref{subsec:routing} and~\ref{subsec:context}).

\subsection{Many kinds of question} \label{subsec:challenge-questions}

``What does PAN propose on bullfighting?'' concerns one party. ``How do PS and
AD differ on taxes?'' compares two. ``Which party proposes most on housing?''
needs all eight but wants a single verdict, and ``What do the right-wing
parties say on immigration?'' names a group rather than parties. One fixed
pipeline serves some of these well and the rest badly. An LLM therefore reads
each question first, and its reading decides how to search and how to lay out
the answer. Letting an agent plan the whole retrieval is left as future work
(Sections~\ref{subsec:queryanalysis}--\ref{subsec:agentic}
and~\ref{subsec:synthesis}).

\section{Challenges of the setting} \label{sec:challenges-setting}
% ============================================================

\subsection{No ground truth} \label{subsec:challenge-groundtruth}

No question-answering dataset exists for Portuguese manifestos, and without one
every design choice rests on a few hand-picked examples. That is exactly how
the failures in Chapter~\ref{ch:proposition} hid for months. Writing a dataset
entirely by hand is slow, while generating it with an LLM risks grading the
model against itself. The middle road was taken. The questions are written by hand; an LLM
proposes the passages that answer each one and a reference answer, and every
proposal is reviewed by hand. Answers are scored by an LLM judge from a
different model family from the generator, with a sample of its verdicts rated
by hand (Sections~\ref{subsec:goldset} and~\ref{subsec:metrics}).

\subsection{A student budget} \label{subsec:challenge-resources}

The thesis had a laptop instead of a GPU server, free-tier services with
strict rate limits for most of its life, and a ten-dollar API key at the end,
while classification and evaluation each need hundreds of LLM calls. The
encoder and reranker therefore run locally, generation uses small open-weight
models served cheaply (free at first, a low-cost paid endpoint from October
2026), every call's cost is logged, and every long job can resume where it
stopped (Sections~\ref{subsec:classification} and~\ref{subsec:llm}). A side effect worth keeping: anyone can run the system.

\section{A moving target} \label{sec:challenges-moving}
% ============================================================

This work ran from 2024 to 2026, while the field changed faster than ever.
That speed was a challenge in its own right, and the one most specific to this
thesis.

\subsection{No settled best practice} \label{subsec:challenge-practices}

How big should a chunk be, and measured in what? Which encoder suits a
non-English retrieval task? Is keyword search still worth keeping? How do you
even evaluate a RAG system? The advice on each changed within months, and a
new technique arrived roughly every other month. More than one choice in this
work followed the best guidance of its day and was outdated by the time it
shipped (Section~\ref{subsec:sota-recent}). Rather than freeze the design or
chase every trend, the system was built for change. Every technique sits behind
a feature flag, a ladder of variants measures what each one adds, and every
reversal is recorded with its reason (Section~\ref{subsec:variants}).

\subsection{Models that outpace the system} \label{subsec:challenge-obsolescence}

The models moved even faster. Within a year, the candidates surveyed at the
start had been overtaken by models that were better, cheaper, and able to read
far longer inputs (Section~\ref{subsec:llm}). Each release raised the same
uncomfortable question: if a frontier model can read every manifesto at once,
why retrieve at all? This thesis treats that as a hypothesis, not a threat.
The case for retrieval is made in Section~\ref{subsec:sota-why-rag}, and RQ5
puts it to the test (Section~\ref{sec:research-questions}).
